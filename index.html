Rectangular fins: heat-transfer enhancement

A narrated worked lecture with 3D geometry. Given → Required → Solution → Discussion. Open the click-through walkthrough.

Open the video directly · Narration: standard ElevenLabs synthetic voice. No music.

Given:

Rectangular aluminum fins: L = 50 mm, t = 0.5 mm, pitch s = 4 mm (250 fins/m). Bare-wall convection coefficient: 40 W/(m²·K). With fins: 30 W/(m²·K).

Required:

Determine the percentage increase in heat transfer, compared at the same wall temperature and ambient-air temperature.

Solution:

Use a 1 m × 1 m wall patch. Assume pure aluminum at 300 K, k = 237 W/(m·K), since conductivity is not given. Use steady 1D fin conduction, perfect attachment, constant properties, negligible radiation and narrow side-edge losses.

1. Count exposed area.

Ab = 1 − Ntw = 0.875 m²
Lc = L + t/2 = 0.05025 m
Af ≈ 2wLc = 0.1005 m²

The corrected length includes the tip area. Fin footprints are removed from the exposed wall area to avoid double counting.

2. Account for cooler fin tips.

m = √(2h/kt) = 22.5018 m⁻¹
ηf = tanh(mLc)/(mLc) = 0.717479

3. Add the fins and exposed wall.

qt = h(NηfAf + Ab)θb
qt = (567.050 W/K)θb
qbare = (40 W/K)θb

4. Compare with the original wall.

Increase = (qt/qbare − 1) × 100
= 1317.6% under the stated assumptions

Discussion:

The handout lists 1640.3%. That value would require ηf ≈ 0.8887 with the same area accounting, instead of 0.7175. The finite-fin and convective-tip calculations agree at approximately 1317.6% for k = 237. A missing or different property/model assumption must be identified before the printed value can be justified.

The conceptual sequence remains: count area, correct only the fin contribution with efficiency, apply the correct convection coefficients, then compare.

Lecture transcript

Given: see the geometry

Let's work through the fin problem in the same order as the lecture. First, look at what we are attaching to the wall. These are thin aluminum plates, not rods. Each one sticks fifty millimeters out from the wall and extends across a width into the page. Rotating the picture makes that width visible. The thickness is only half a millimeter. I've exaggerated it here so we can see it. Heat travels from the warm wall into each plate, then leaves its exposed surfaces for the cooler air. The purpose of a fin is to give heat more surface from which to escape.

Required: compare two heat rates

We want the percentage increase in heat transfer. That means the new heat rate, minus the original heat rate, divided by the original. The comparison uses the same wall temperature and the same air temperature. Their difference is theta sub b in the lecture. We don't need its numerical value, because it will cancel. Notice that the convection coefficient changes: forty for the bare wall, but thirty after the fins are attached. We must use both values. The new heat rate will include two contributions: heat from all the fins, and heat from the exposed wall between them.

Solution: choose a representative patch

Let's choose a wall patch one meter high and one meter wide. This is just a convenient counting area. The answer is a percentage, so the patch size will cancel. There are two hundred and fifty fins per meter of height. That confirms that four millimeters is the repeat spacing, or pitch. It includes one fin thickness plus one open gap. The clear gap is therefore three and a half millimeters. The width of each fin is one meter, into the page. We will use the letter w for that width. Now we can count surfaces without needing the dimensions of an entire real wall.

Solution: the exposed wall area

Before adding fins, our patch has one square meter of exposed wall. A fin covers a narrow strip where its base touches the wall. That covered strip is thickness times width. Multiply by two hundred and fifty fins, and the total covered area is zero point one two five square meters. Subtract it from one. The wall area still exposed to air is zero point eight seven five square meters. The lecture calls this the prime surface area, A sub b. This subtraction matters: the covered area cannot also lose heat directly to the air. We must not count it twice.

Solution: the area of one fin

Now isolate one fin. These two broad faces provide almost all of its exposed area. Each face is length times width. The narrow tip adds thickness times width. We neglect the very narrow side edges in this wide-fin model. So the area of one fin is two L w, plus t w. The lecture packages this neatly using a corrected length: L sub c equals L plus t over two. Then the fin area is two w L sub c. This does not mean the actual plate got longer. It is a convenient way to include the small tip area in the model.

Solution: why a fin needs an efficiency

It is tempting to multiply all that new area by the wall's temperature difference. But that would assume every point on the fin stays as hot as its base. Watch what happens as we move out along the fin. Heat has already escaped from the surfaces, and the aluminum has a finite resistance to conduction. The temperature falls toward the tip. A cooler surface transfers less heat per unit area. Fin efficiency corrects for this. It is actual fin heat transfer divided by the ideal heat transfer if the entire fin were at the base temperature. It is not a fraction of heat that disappears. At steady state, heat entering the fin leaves through its surfaces.

Solution: calculate the fin efficiency

Here is the one new calculation we need. For a wide, thin rectangular fin, m is the square root of two h divided by k t. The question does not supply conductivity or a temperature for selecting it. We will explicitly assume pure aluminum at three hundred kelvin, using the appendix value of two hundred and thirty-seven watts per meter kelvin. With h equal to thirty, m is twenty-two point five inverse meters. Multiply by the corrected length to get one point one three one. The finite-fin efficiency is the hyperbolic tangent of that number, divided by the number itself. The result is zero point seven one seven five, or about seventy-two percent.

Solution: combine the two heat contributions

We can now use the lecture's array equation. Start with the number of fins, times the efficiency of each fin, times its exposed area. That is the fins' effective area. Then add the exposed wall area, without a fin-efficiency penalty, because the wall is at the base temperature. The bracket becomes eighteen point nine zero two square meters. Multiplying by the finned-wall convection coefficient, thirty, gives a heat rate of five hundred and sixty-seven point zero five times theta sub b. The bare wall heat rate is forty times theta sub b. Now the temperature difference cancels, exactly as promised.

Solution: turn the ratio into a percentage

Divide the two heat rates. The finned wall transfers fourteen point one seven six times as much heat as the bare wall under our assumptions. But the question asks for the increase, not the new total. One of those copies is the heat transfer we already had. Subtract one, then multiply by one hundred. The increase is thirteen hundred and seventeen point six percent. The sequence is simple: count the exposed area, correct the fin area for its lower temperature, apply the changed convection coefficient, and compare with the original wall.

Discussion: check the answer and assumptions

There is one important issue with the handout. It prints sixteen hundred and forty point three percent. That is not the result of the lecture's finite-fin equations with the aluminum property we used. The printed value would require a fin efficiency of about eighty-nine percent, instead of seventy-two percent, with the same areas. The question leaves conductivity unspecified, so we should state our assumption and flag the mismatch, rather than change the arithmetic to fit the answer. The method is still the lesson: fins plus exposed wall, with efficiency applied only to the fins. This is the calculation to understand and the property assumption to check with your instructor.
